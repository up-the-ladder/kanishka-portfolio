Relay

Distributed job execution platform in Go · In development

Go · gRPC · PostgreSQL · Redis · Docker · Kubernetes

At a glance

  • Runs asynchronous jobs across many concurrent workers without losing or double-executing work.
  • PostgreSQL is the single source of truth for job state; claiming is transactional, so two workers cannot own the same job.
  • Recovery is built in: leases expire, heartbeats detect dead workers, and retries are idempotent with exponential backoff.
  • Redis and Kubernetes are only introduced where they solve a demonstrated need, not by default.
  • Failure testing, load testing, observability, and reproducible benchmarks are the next milestone. Performance numbers are not claimed until they are measured.

Problem

A service that needs to hand off work, whether that is encoding a file, running a pipeline, or sending a batch, has to answer awkward questions the moment more than one worker exists. Which worker owns this job? What happens if that worker dies halfway through? If the job is retried, does the side effect happen twice? Relay exists to work through those questions directly rather than delegating them to a managed queue.

Motivation

Most of the difficulty in a job system is not scheduling; it is ownership and recovery. Building it from the database upward makes the hard parts visible: transactional claiming, lease expiry, detection of a worker that stopped without saying so, and recovery paths that are safe to run more than once.

Constraints

  • Failure is assumed, not exceptional. A worker may crash, stall, or be partitioned at any point in a job’s lifetime.
  • No job may be executed by two workers concurrently, and no job may be silently abandoned.
  • Retries must be safe, which means execution has to be idempotent.
  • The system must be reproducible locally, so a reviewer can run it rather than take the design on trust.
  • Infrastructure is added only when a specific problem justifies it.

Architecture

Clients submit jobs over gRPC, which gives a typed contract between submitters and the platform. Submitted jobs land in PostgreSQL, which holds durable job state and acts as the coordination point for the whole system. Workers claim jobs from PostgreSQL under a transaction, so claiming is concurrency-safe by construction rather than by convention, and each claim carries a lease.

Client → gRPC → job record in PostgreSQL → transactional claim by a worker → lease held and refreshed by heartbeat → completion, retry with backoff, or lease expiry and re-claim.

Workers heartbeat while they hold a lease. A lease that stops being refreshed is the signal that the owning worker is gone, which lets the job be returned to the pool and picked up again without an external coordinator making that decision.

Failure model

  • Worker dies mid-job. Its lease expires; the job becomes claimable again.
  • Worker stalls. Heartbeats stop, which is treated the same way as a death.
  • Two workers race for the same job. Transactional claiming means exactly one wins.
  • Job fails. It is retried with exponential backoff rather than immediately, to avoid hammering a failing dependency.
  • Job is retried after partial execution. Idempotency keeps the re-run safe.
  • Process is asked to shut down. Graceful shutdown releases or completes in-flight work rather than dropping it.

Engineering decisions

PostgreSQL as the coordination layer rather than a queue broker. Job state needs to be durable and queryable, and transactional claiming gives mutual exclusion with the same mechanism that gives durability. One system, one source of truth.

Leases plus heartbeats rather than timeouts alone. A fixed timeout either kills legitimate long jobs or leaves dead ones stuck. A refreshed lease lets a long job stay owned while still expiring quickly when the owner disappears.

Idempotency before retries. Retry logic is only safe if re-execution is safe, so idempotency is a property of the execution path rather than a guard bolted on afterwards.

Redis and Kubernetes kept out of the critical path by default. Both are available, but the system is designed so they are introduced where they solve a demonstrated need, not so the stack looks larger. Docker covers reproducible local deployment.

Evaluation

The measurement work is the next milestone rather than a finished result: failure testing (killing workers mid-job and asserting recovery), load testing, observability, and reproducible performance benchmarks. Throughput and latency figures belong here once they come from a benchmark someone else can re-run, so they are not claimed yet.

Tradeoffs

  • Coordinating through PostgreSQL keeps the design simple and consistent, but puts claim throughput on the database rather than on a purpose-built broker.
  • Short leases recover from failures faster but add heartbeat traffic; long leases do the opposite.
  • Requiring idempotent execution pushes work onto job authors in exchange for safe retries.
  • Deferring Redis and Kubernetes keeps the system understandable, at the cost of not yet demonstrating behaviour at scale.

What’s next

  • Fault-injection suite: kill workers at each lifecycle stage and assert no job is lost or duplicated.
  • Load tests with reproducible scripts and published numbers.
  • Metrics and tracing across submission, claim, execution, and retry.
